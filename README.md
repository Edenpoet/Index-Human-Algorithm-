# Index-Human-Algorithm
## Cognition Illustrations: An Exploratory Record of Handmade Visual Pattern Creation

**Project:** Index-Human-Algorithm  
**Collection:** Cognition Illustrations  
**Creator:** Project creator  
**Research format:** Exploratory, first-person creative-process documentation  
**Collection status at time of drafting:** Approximately 356 retained patterns  
**Initial milestone:** 1,000 retained patterns

---

## Abstract

*Index-Human-Algorithm* documents an ongoing collection of handmade visual patterns created by one person using a pen and individual index cards. The creator describes the process as intuitive and freehand: patterns are made from scratch without rulers or measured construction. The creator does not begin every card from a fixed template and reports sometimes arranging earlier cards where they can be seen while making new work. Completed attempts are judged personally; cards that do not meet the creator's own standard may be discarded, while selected patterns are retained.

The project is not presented as a scientific measurement of brain activity, a model of neural pathways, or a validated theory of cognition. Its purpose is to preserve the creator's actual work and document the conditions and decisions that the creator can reliably report. The photographs supplied for this draft show a varied body of visual constructions, including radial forms, branching lines, stepped contours, connected points, nested outlines, and layered petal-like structures. These descriptions are visual observations of the drawings, not explanations of their neurological meaning.

At the time of drafting, the creator reports approximately 356 retained patterns and an initial goal of reaching 1,000. The archive is intended to grow as the work continues.

## 1. Introduction

This project began as a repeated practice of making patterns by hand on index cards. Each card holds an individual construction. The cards are made with a pen, without rulers or measured dimensions. The creator describes beginning with a blank card and allowing the drawing to develop through the act of drawing itself.

The project takes its name, *Index-Human-Algorithm*, and uses the collection title *Cognition Illustrations*. These names belong to the creator's project vocabulary. They do not, by themselves, establish that the drawings are algorithms, that they encode neural activity, or that they represent a scientific map of cognition.

The central subject of this documentation is the creator's process and the growing set of physical artifacts. The paper therefore emphasizes what can be directly reported or observed: materials, working conditions, selection of retained cards, visible characteristics of the drawings, and the organization of the archive.

## 2. Purpose and Scope

The purpose of the project is to create and preserve a substantial corpus of original, handmade visual patterns while documenting the creator's own account of making them.

The current scope is intentionally limited:

- Preserve the original patterns as individual works.
- Record identifying numbers and photographs where available.
- Describe visible features without assigning unsupported meanings.
- Document the creator's process in the creator's own terms.
- Maintain a growing archive, with 1,000 retained patterns as the initial milestone.

This is an exploratory record of one person's creative practice. It is not a controlled experiment, a clinical assessment, or a claim that the patterns reveal universal properties of human cognition.

## 3. Materials and Working Conditions

The creator reports using:

- Individual index cards.
- A pen.
- Freehand drawing.
- No rulers or measured dimensions.

The creator describes starting with a fresh, blank index card and beginning the drawing without needing a predetermined finished design. The hand's movement is an important part of the experience: the creator allows the drawing to develop while making it.

The creator sometimes places previously completed cards in view. Seeing earlier work can be part of the drawing environment, although this does not happen every time. The available account does not establish that earlier cards are copied or consulted according to a fixed procedure.

## 4. Creation, Selection, and Retention

Not every attempt becomes part of the archive. The creator reports that some cards are discarded when a drawing does not turn out as desired. A pattern is retained when the creator considers it good enough to keep.

This means the existing collection is a selected archive, not a complete record of every drawing attempt. The selection standard is personal and belongs to the creator. The project should preserve this fact rather than describe the collection as a complete or unbiased sample of all attempts.

The creator's account does not currently specify a formal scoring system, a fixed number of attempts per session, or a standardized definition of a successful pattern. Those details should remain unspecified unless the creator chooses to document them later.

## 5. Visual Observations in the Supplied Pattern Photographs

The photographs show many individual cards arranged together, as well as closer views of selected cards. The following descriptions refer only to visible characteristics in those photographs. They are descriptive groupings for discussing the archive, not formal categories imposed on every card. A single drawing may combine several of these features.

### 5.1 Radial and center-oriented constructions

A number of drawings are organized around a visible central point or crossing. Lines, shapes, or repeated elements extend outward from that area. Some compositions have four prominent directions; others divide the surrounding space into more numerous sectors.

In several examples, the center acts as a meeting point for straight lines and diagonals. In others, it is surrounded by repeated lobes or petal-like forms. The resulting compositions often have a balanced or approximately balanced appearance, although the hand-drawn lines and outlines are not mechanically identical.

### 5.2 Branching and stepped extensions

Some patterns extend outward through sequences of short turns. These create stair-step, angular, or branch-like paths. The extensions may repeat around a center or appear in opposing sections of a composition.

The photographs show both relatively open versions, where the branches remain separated, and denser versions, where the stepped outlines build into a larger perimeter. This description concerns the visible path of the lines; it does not imply that the drawings represent actual neural branches or biological structures.

### 5.3 Connected points and line networks

Several cards include darkened dots or small circular marks connected by straight lines. In some examples, the points sit at endpoints; in others, they appear at intersections or along repeated paths. Some networks form cross-like arrangements, while others create more complex polygonal or lattice-like structures.

The dots and connecting lines are visible graphic elements. Their presence can be recorded consistently in the archive without assigning them a specific scientific meaning.

### 5.4 Nested outlines and layered boundaries

Other drawings contain multiple outlines arranged around a central form. These layers may be angular, rounded, irregular, or a combination. In some cards, a smaller central construction is surrounded by a larger stepped or polygonal boundary.

The repeated outlines create visual depth through accumulation of contours on a flat card. The photographs also show places where lines overlap, diverge, or are reinforced by additional pen strokes.

### 5.5 Petal-like and repeated-lobe constructions

Some patterns use repeated pointed or rounded forms arranged around a center. These forms can resemble petals or leaves as a matter of visual description. Some cards contain one ring of lobes; others show several layers, with smaller forms near the center and larger forms toward the outside.

The number, size, and outline of the lobes vary between cards. These are observations about shape and arrangement, not claims that the drawings depict botanical objects.

### 5.6 Geometric divisions and cross forms

The photographs also include crosses, diamonds, squares, triangles, and other angular divisions. Some are relatively sparse, consisting of a few intersecting lines. Others contain internal subdivisions, repeated edges, or additional outlines.

A recurring visible feature is the use of a central crossing with extensions in four directions. The surrounding construction may remain simple or become more elaborate through added boundaries, diagonals, dots, or stepped edges.

### 5.7 Variation in density and line treatment

The cards vary in how much of the available space is occupied. Some are open, with a small number of long lines and large areas of blank card. Others are dense, containing many short strokes, repeated shapes, intersections, or layered contours.

Line weight also varies. Some marks are light and fine; others are darker, thicker, or reinforced. In some examples, dark dots create visual emphasis at selected points. These differences can be documented as features of the physical drawings without assuming why they occurred.

### 5.8 Combinations rather than fixed types

The visual groupings above are not mutually exclusive. A single card may combine a central cross, radial lobes, stepped outer boundaries, and darkened points. Another may combine branching paths with a geometric perimeter.

For that reason, the archive should avoid forcing every pattern into one exclusive category. Descriptive tags can be added when useful, while preserving the full image as the primary record.

## 6. What the Photographs Support—and What They Do Not

The supplied photographs support a description of a substantial collection of handmade cards and a range of visible construction styles. They show recurring visual features such as central organization, radial extension, repeated forms, angular boundaries, connected points, and layered outlines.

The photographs do not establish the creator's moment-to-moment thoughts, the exact sequence of hand movements, the time taken to make each card, or the reason a particular line was chosen. Those details can only be included when the creator reports them.

Likewise, visual resemblance to networks, pathways, flowers, or geometric systems should not be treated as proof that a drawing represents a neural structure, biological process, mathematical model, or computational algorithm.

The project keeps a clear boundary between:
1. **Creator-reported process:** what the creator says about making and selecting the cards.
2. **Visible description:** what can be seen in the photographs.
3. **Interpretation or hypothesis:** ideas that may be explored later but are not established facts.

## 7. Archive Documentation

Each retained card should be treated as an individual artifact. A practical record may include:

| Field | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Pattern ID | A stable identifier, such as IC-001 |
| Image | Original photograph or scan |
| Creation date | Date, if known |
| Archive date | Date the digital record was added |
| Materials | Pen and index card, or other accurate details |
| Creator's note | Optional first-person description |
| Visual description | Neutral description of visible features |
| Status | Retained, if included in the archive |

The archive should distinguish a card's creation date from the date it was photographed or uploaded. If a date is unknown, it should be left unknown rather than estimated.

Original image files should be preserved where possible. Edited, cropped, or enhanced versions may be included as secondary copies, but should not silently replace the original documentation.

## 8. Current Collection and Initial Milestone

The creator reports approximately **356 retained patterns** at the time this draft was prepared. The initial milestone is **1,000 retained patterns**, leaving approximately 644 to reach that target if the current count is accurate.

The photographs include broad arrangements of many cards and close views of examples. The broad photographs communicate the scale and variety of the collection, while the close views make individual line structures easier to inspect. The photographs are illustrative documentation and are not a verified inventory of every retained card.

The count should be updated by the creator as the archive is cataloged. The paper should display a date alongside future counts so that changes can be understood over time.

## 9. Limitations

This document is based on the creator's account and the photographs supplied for the project. It does not report a controlled study, independent replication, participant comparison, neurological measurement, or formal assessment of drawing skill.

The project does not claim that the patterns are direct representations of brain activity or neural pathways. The phrase *Cognition Illustrations* is the creator's project title and conceptual language, not a scientific finding.

The current archive is selected by the creator. Because unsuccessful or unwanted attempts may be discarded, the retained cards do not represent every drawing attempt. The available photographs also do not provide a complete, individually verified inventory.

These limitations are part of an honest description of the project and can be updated if the documentation process becomes more systematic.

## 10. Future Development

The immediate goal is to continue creating and preserving patterns toward the 1,000-card milestone. Future development may include:

- Individual photographs or scans for each retained card.
- Consistent pattern identifiers.
- Dates where they are known.
- Optional creator notes recorded close to the time of creation.
- Neutral visual descriptions.
- A chronological index of the archive.
- Documentation of changes to the process, if the creator notices or reports them.

Future analysis may compare visible features across the collection. Any such analysis should explain how patterns were selected and described, and should distinguish observation from interpretation.

## 11. Conclusion

*Index-Human-Algorithm: Cognition Illustrations* is an ongoing record of handmade visual pattern creation. The creator makes patterns on blank index cards using a pen and freehand construction, without rulers or measured dimensions. The creator sometimes views earlier cards while making new ones and personally decides which attempts are worth retaining.

The photographs show a varied collection of radial, branching, geometric, layered, and interconnected visual constructions. These features can be described and preserved without claiming that they reveal specific neurological processes.

The project's contribution at this stage is the original body of work and its careful documentation: the cards themselves, the creator's account of the process, and the archive that preserves the collection as it grows toward its initial goal of 1,000 patterns.
